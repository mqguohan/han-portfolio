Project Overview

LANDPLAY is an interactive, rule-based landscape simulation that explores how human actions shape evolving environments.

Players make decisions that change local ecological conditions, while the system responds through a set of interconnected rules. Each action leaves traces, and these changes accumulate over time, influencing how the landscape develops.

Rather than designing a landscape with a predetermined outcome, players interact with a system that produces different possibilities through their decisions, environmental feedback, and uncertainty.

02

Concept

A landscape is not a fixed object, but a system of changing conditions, interactions, and accumulated histories.

In LANDPLAY, human intervention becomes one of many forces shaping this system. Each decision changes certain conditions, but its consequences depend on what already exists and how different elements interact. Small changes may accumulate, unexpected patterns may emerge, and similar actions may lead to different outcomes.

Rather than presenting an ideal landscape to achieve, LANDPLAY allows different ecological conditions to develop without defining a single correct ending. Growth, decline, recovery, and artificial stability are all possible results of the same evolving system.

03

How It Works

LANDPLAY operates through a continuous feedback loop between player actions and environmental conditions.

Each action is interpreted through the landscape's current state and a set of ecological rules, producing changes that become part of the system's memory. These accumulated changes influence future responses, allowing different environmental patterns to emerge over time.

At the end of each playthrough, the system interprets the resulting ecological conditions and significant memories, translating them into a final reading and a synthetic landscape.

04

Gameplay Structure

LANDPLAY unfolds through four stages, moving from an initial landscape to a final synthetic world.

  1. 01

    Start & Initial Land

    Players begin by selecting an initial landscape condition. Each starting environment provides a different set of conditions for the simulation.

  2. 02

    Main Simulation

    Players interact with the landscape through a series of interventions. The environment responds to each action, while previous changes continue to influence how the system develops.

  3. 03

    Final Reading

    At the end of the simulation, the system produces a reading of the landscape's ecological condition and significant memories, summarizing the changes accumulated throughout the playthrough.

  4. 04

    Ending World

    The final reading is translated into a synthetic landscape, offering a visual interpretation of the environment shaped by the player's decisions and the system's responses.

    This visualization uses a predefined world asset. Its displayed metrics are asset presets, not measurements from this playthrough.

05

Behind the System

LANDPLAY is built around a set of ecological conditions, rules, and accumulated memories. Each intervention changes the environment, but its consequences depend on the state of the landscape and what has happened before.

The system is not designed to predict real ecological processes. Instead, it creates a simplified environment in which different conditions and interventions can interact, producing both expected and unexpected results.

01 — Rules & Ecological States

The landscape consists of 25 cells, each carrying ten environmental conditions. These conditions interact through thresholds, feedback rules, and occasional random events, producing 16 possible dominant ecological states.

An intervention does not always produce a single predictable effect. For example, rainfall can support vegetation growth, but it can also help contamination spread through an already polluted environment.

The same action can therefore produce different consequences depending on the conditions in which it takes place.

02 — Environmental Memory

In LANDPLAY, environmental change does not disappear after an action ends. The system retains traces of previous interventions, including extraction, contamination, protection, and recovery.

For example, extraction leaves a persistent mark that can continue to weaken a landscape's stability. Recovery can gradually reduce this effect, but the record of extraction remains.

Memory allows the landscape to carry its own history. Its future depends not only on its current appearance, but also on the interventions it has experienced.

03 — From Conditions to Outcomes

At the end of each playthrough, LANDPLAY evaluates the accumulated ecological conditions, including stability, biodiversity, toxicity, artificiality, and hidden risks.

These values contribute to the selection of one of twelve ending types, each representing a different interpretation of the evolving landscape.

A landscape may appear visually abundant while remaining unstable or contaminated. Similarly, a less spectacular landscape may have developed greater ecological resilience.

The final reading brings together these conditions and significant memories, translating them into a written interpretation and a generative world prompt. A corresponding synthetic landscape is then presented as the ending world.

06

Ending Worlds

Each LANDPLAY session leads to one of twelve possible ending worlds, selected through the accumulated ecological conditions, environmental memories, and the system's classification rules.

These worlds represent different environmental trajectories, ranging from recovery and ecological growth to extraction, artificial control, and collapse. Rather than presenting a single ideal outcome, LANDPLAY allows different conditions and histories to produce distinct landscape interpretations.

The visual appearance of an ending world does not necessarily reflect its ecological health. A landscape may appear abundant while remaining fragile, unstable, or shaped by hidden damage.

A beautiful landscape is not necessarily a healthy one.

In LANDPLAY, visual appearance and ecological condition do not always tell the same story.

07

Reflection

LANDPLAY explores a different way of approaching landscape design: rather than directly shaping a final form, I designed the conditions and rules through which landscapes could evolve.

Through this process, I became more interested in the relationship between intervention and consequence. A landscape may appear to recover while still carrying traces of previous damage, or become visually abundant without necessarily being ecologically stable. These contradictions are part of what makes the system interesting to me.

LANDPLAY is not intended to accurately predict ecological change, but to make complex environmental relationships more accessible through interaction and play. Moving forward, I want to explore how systems like this might support environmental learning, allowing people to experiment with ecological decisions and reflect on their consequences.
